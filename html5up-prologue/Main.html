Rewinding Times

William "Dummy" Hoy

Born May 23, 1862, William Hoy, nicknamed "Dummy" for his deafness, dreamed from his shoe repair shop in rural Ohio to one day be an MLB player. Despite his nickname, Hoy was hardly a "Dummy" (I know this implies deafness not his intellect), graduating valedictiorian from his high school. Luckily for baseball fans, his ample time spent on the diamond during the weekends earned him a spot in the big leagues. Despite his short career, he actually achieved impressive milestones, such as most MLB games as a CF (broken by Tris Speaker), and recorded 2000 hits. Most importantly, Hoy is credited with a signaling system for balls and strikes (although debated its accepted he invented the concept of signaling what the result was). The Reds HOF inductee died at the age of ninety-nine, but not before leaving an incalculable impact on baseball history. Hall of Fame snub as a Pioneer inductee by the way.

Project Goals

Umpire signals are here to stay; however, with the advent of the MLB PitchCom System, deaf pitchers face an extreme disadvantage by the constriction of the pitch clock and inability to hear the call. The lack of a solution to this issue is notable, seeing as how youth participation in baseball is more of a focus than ever. Additionally, while thinking about ways to fix or better this issue, I realized that the current PitchCom System is very, very cumbersome, enigmatic, and insecure. A heads-up-display (HUD) was the best practical solution in my opinion, especially with the rapid-growing field of wearable technology such as mixed reality glasses. For the signaling, a display system with custom batter fitting and estimated-umpire-zoning (the latter being a self-proof) seemed way more suited for the field. Note that the glasses are a concept and can be bettered for MLB use; however, I'm not that liquid (this is not WhoCaresAboutABudget).
